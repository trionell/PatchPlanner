PatchPlanner · Slice 41 · design review

The plot on paper

One stage plot, three orthographic views, one page each, at one stated scale. Drawn below from the Kyrkan plot in the live database — the real elements, the real layer colours, the real 600 cm truss yawed 32° and raked 31°, run through the same projection the editor uses. Nothing here is sketched.

The three sheets

A4 landscape. The drawing frame sits at the same place on all three, so the views are registered: a vertical dropped from the plan lands on the same element in the front elevation, and the front and side elevations share a floor datum. That registration is the argument for one fixed scale — it is worth more than filling each page.

Crossing from the dark canvas onto white paper

Layer colour is the plot's whole palette — elements carry none of their own. Every one of these values was picked to glow on a near-black canvas, and that is exactly what makes them fail on paper. The rule below is what this slice writes into design/, and it is wider than this one document.

Inverting the lightness is the wrong mechanism, and this plot proves it

The roadmap says a near-grey gets its lightness inverted. Venue is a near-grey — #5e5c64, 4% saturation — sitting at L 37.6%. It is one of the two layers on this plot that already reads perfectly well on paper, at 6.58:1. Inverting it gives L 62.4%, #9d9ba3, and 2.74:1: the rule would take the only layer that needed no help and be the single thing that broke it. Lowering lightness to a contrast floor does what inversion was reaching for — a light grey still comes out dark, which is what the wording was describing — and it correctly does nothing at all to a colour that is already dark enough.

Keep the hue, lower the lightness

Hue and saturation are what the planner chose and what tells one layer from another. Lightness is what the ground dictated. So crossing to paper preserves H and S and lowers L until the ink clears 4.5:1 against white — and stops there. A colour already dark enough is left untouched.

No colour chosen prints as ink

An unset layer is drawn #a1a1aa on screen because something had to read on black. That is not a decision, so paper does not honour it as one: an unset layer prints in the document's own ink. This is also what stops the mapped grey from colliding with a real grey layer.

A floor at L 12%

Lowering lightness without a floor turns a deep blue into the same black as the frame and the lettering. The clamp keeps a coloured line legible as a coloured line.

Complete in monochrome

§8's standing rule survives: every layer is named in the key, every fixture is numbered into a schedule. Photocopy the sheet and nothing is lost but the speed of reading it.

What the mockup decides

Each of these is a call this drawing makes. They become the change's requirements once approved.

  1. One document is one plot, not one event.An event holds several plots (Kyrkan and Festlokal here). The export hangs off the plot: GET /events/{id}/stage-plots/{plotId}/export/pdf, filename <event>-<plot>-plot.pdf. That also removes today's trap, where the printed sheet silently depends on which view the editor was left on.
  2. A4 landscape, three pages, in the order top → front → side.A stage is wider than it is deep and much wider than it is tall; portrait wastes the axis the drawing needs. Page one carries the masthead — the same block the audio document uses, with PLOT where it says AUDIO. Pages two and three carry the running head.
  3. One scale for all three views, chosen from a ladder and stated in figures.The largest of 1:20, 1:25, 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:250, 1:500 at which every view fits the common frame. This plot lands on 1:100, set by the top view. A scale bar is drawn beside the figure, because a photocopier resizes the sheet and the bar resizes with it while the words do not.
  4. Three shared windows, so the views register.The X window (stage width) is shared by the top and front pages; the Z window (height) by front and side; the Y window (depth) is the top page's vertical and the side page's horizontal. One projection set, not three independent drawings.
  5. The grid is drawn at a stepped-up multiple, and says so.25 cm at 1:100 is a 2.5 mm mesh — noise. The sheet draws the smallest multiple of the plot's grid that measures at least 8 mm on paper and states both: Grid 25 cm · drawn every 4th (100 cm).
  6. A title block, not a caption.Right-hand column, full height: scale and bar, grid, the layer key, the truss schedule, and the conventions. It is a key to that page, so it repeats on all three — a rigger pulls out one sheet and it still reads.
  7. The floor is a datum, not a dashed line.Elevations get a solid 0.4 mm rule at z = 0 with hatch ticks beneath it, which is how every trade draws ground. Dashes stay reserved for what is unresolved.
  8. A truss fixture is a numbered balloon; its name lives in the schedule.At 1:100 a fixture is 3 mm across and ADJ, ULTRA LB18 · FID 1101 · 2.001 is 22 mm of type. So the drawing carries a numbered circle at the fixture's true point on the bar, and the title block carries the number against the name, FID, DMX and offset. The number is the join.
  9. Nothing that carries meaning is typed.Inherited from Slice 40 and non-negotiable: the document is built-in Helvetica through a cp1252 translator, and an unrepresentable character comes out as a full stop rather than as visible damage. The scale bar, the hatching, the balloons and the north/stage-front arrow are all vectors.
  10. The glyphs move to one data file both sides read. lib/stagePlotIcons.tsx is 627 lines of SVG for 17 icons × 3 views, and the backend cannot call TypeScript. The path data becomes a single embedded data file; the TSX becomes a renderer of it, and Go grows a small renderer of the same subset (M, L, C, A, Z, plus rect and circle) onto the page. It is a real refactor and the only option where a glyph edited for the screen cannot silently stop matching the paper.
  11. A fourth sheet carries the element schedule.Grouped by layer, under the layer's own paper ink, with the truss's bar and fixtures as continuation lines. It is what somebody marking out a floor carries, and it is where the numbered balloons on sheets 1–3 resolve to names.
  12. A plot too large for 1:500 tiles rather than refuses.The ladder bottoms out at 1:500; past that the drawing divides across pages the way Slice 40's flow drawing does — cut between elements and never through one, with each tile stating its position in the set. The scale stays fixed and stated, which is the whole point: tiles of one drawing at one scale can be laid side by side on a floor and read as one plot.

Carried forward, not settled here

  • The legibility floor is still a guess.Slice 40 set 5 pt as the size below which a drawing tiles instead of shrinking, and no festival-sized plan has been through it in either slice. Tiling now has two callers and one untested threshold.
